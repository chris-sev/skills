# Make It Skim

## The stack

Make It Skim is the structure layer of a three-part writing stack:

1. **unslop** removes AI filler and hedging.
2. **humanizer** makes the sentences sound like a person wrote them.
3. **make-it-skim** (this skill) arranges those sentences so a skimming eye lands on what matters.

Clean sentences in a wall of text still fail. Use all three for anything a human will read.

## The research this is built on

Nielsen Norman Group has tracked how people read on screens since 1997. The findings have held through every change in screens and connection speeds:

- **79% scanned, 16% read.** In the classic study, 79% of users scanned every new page they came across. Only 16% read word by word. (Morkes and Nielsen, 1997)
- **Layout alone is worth 47%.** Five versions of the same content with the same facts: a scannable layout scored 47% higher on measured usability than the promotional original. Concise text scored 58% higher. Objective, hype-free language scored 27% higher. All three combined scored 124% higher.
- **Readers follow four scanning patterns.** Across five eyetracking studies, 500+ participants, and 750+ hours of sessions, NN/g found four main patterns. Worst to best: the F-pattern, the spotted pattern, the layer-cake pattern, and the commitment pattern.

The patterns are the design targets of this skill:

- **F-pattern (the failure mode).** Readers skim the top lines, then scan down the left edge, reading less of each line. It appears when text has no formatting: no bolding, no bullets, no subheadings. Readers miss large chunks and do not know they missed them. A wall of text forces the F-pattern.
- **Spotted pattern (anchors work).** Readers fixate on things that look different: bold words, links, digits, lists, and words that match what they are hunting for. Numbers, names, and dates are natural anchors.
- **Layer-cake pattern (the goal).** Readers scan the headings, then dive into the body text under the heading that looks relevant. It is the most effective scanning pattern. It only works when headings are easy to spot, descriptive, and honest about what the section contains.
- **Commitment pattern (the exception).** Motivated readers sometimes read almost everything. Even they comprehend better when the content is chunked with clear subheadings. Never use this pattern as an excuse for a wall of text.

Design every piece of writing so the reader lands in layer-cake, not F.

## Rules

1. **Hook line first.** Open with the single most important line, standing alone: the answer, the news, or the ask. No warm-up, no background before the point. (The inverted pyramid: conclusion first, details after, background last.)
2. **One idea per line or paragraph.** Paragraphs stay at one to three short sentences. A second idea buried mid-paragraph is invisible to a scanner. Split it out or cut it.
3. **Signpost every section.** Each block starts with a bold lead phrase or a short heading that says what the block is. Headings must be descriptive, never clever: a scanner should get the whole story from the headings alone. Lead each heading with its most information-bearing words, because the first two words carry the gist when the rest is skipped.
4. **Lists over prose.** Anything enumerable (stats, steps, questions, options, reasons) becomes bullets or a numbered list, never a comma chain inside a paragraph.
5. **Front-load every list item.** The first three words of a bullet decide whether the rest gets read. Put the point first, the explanation after. Do not open consecutive bullets with the same words: readers bypass repeated openings and skip those items entirely.
6. **Bold anchors only.** Bold the numbers, names, dates, and the one key phrase per bullet that carries the point. Bold is what the spotted pattern lands on. If everything is bold, nothing is.
7. **Numbers beat adjectives.** "2,865 images generated" scans. "Lots of engagement" does not. Hype also costs trust: readers spend effort filtering boastful claims instead of believing them.
8. **White space is structure.** A blank line between blocks is a signpost. Dense text reads as effort, and effort gets skipped.
9. **Questions get their own lines.** Numbered, bolded lead, one per line. A question inside a paragraph is a question that never gets answered.
10. **Close with the ask on its own line.** If the reader does only one thing, it should be findable in under two seconds. Never let the ask trail off at the end of a paragraph.

## The skim test

Before sending anything human-facing, re-read it the way a scanner does:

1. **Layer-cake test.** Read only the headings and bold lead phrases, in order. They should tell the whole story.
2. **Skim test.** Read only the hook line, the bold anchors, the first three words of every bullet, and the closing line.

If those fragments alone carry the full message and the ask, ship it. If the point lives anywhere else, restructure until it does.

## What this is not

- Not marketing hype. Scannability is signposting, not adjectives. NN/g measured objective language beating promotional language by 27%. The voice stays plain and human (unslop and humanizer still apply).
- Not an excuse to cut substance. Cut repetition and filler, never the facts, caveats, or numbers the reader needs.
- Not for code, data tables, or machine-facing text.
